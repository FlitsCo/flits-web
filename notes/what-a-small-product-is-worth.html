What a Small Product Is Worth

Building a product from nothing takes a year. Buying a working one takes a month and a wire transfer. How we choose, and what we are willing to pay.

Manuel AsbeckSeptember 2026

There are two ways to end up owning a small software business. You build one, which takes about a year and works maybe a third of the time. Or you buy one that already works, which takes about a month and a wire transfer. We do both, and people keep asking how we decide, so here is the actual reasoning rather than the tidy version.

The year you skip

When you build something new, the first year is not really spent building. It is spent finding out whether anyone wants it. The code is the cheap part. The expensive part is the twelve months of not knowing, and the fact that most of the time the answer at the end of it is no.

Buying skips that. If a product has been charging people for three years, the question of whether anyone wants it is already answered, in the most credible way it can be answered, which is that they paid. That is most of what you are actually buying. Not the code. The code is usually the worst part of the deal.

What the multiple is really pricing

Small products change hands for a few times their annual profit. The number moves around depending on how the revenue arrives and how much of it survives the founder leaving, but that is the neighbourhood.

It is tempting to argue about whether the number is high or low. That is the wrong argument. A multiple is just a statement about how many years you expect the thing to keep earning. Pay three times profit and you are saying you think it lasts a lot longer than three more years. Pay six and you are making a much braver claim about a piece of software you have known for two weeks.

So the real question is never whether the price is cheap. It is: what has to stay true for this to still be earning in five years, and do I believe that? Usually a couple of things have to stay true, and usually at least one of them is out of your hands.

What we check before we wire anything

Four things, in roughly this order. None of them is about the code.

Where do the customers come from? If they arrive through search, word of mouth, or an integration nobody controls, good. If they all arrive through one channel that belongs to somebody else, we are not buying a business, we are buying a position in someone else's, and that position can be revoked.

What breaks when the founder walks away? Often quite a lot. Support answers that only exist in their head, one server nobody else has ever logged into, a customer relationship that was really a friendship. The polite version of this question is "what is undocumented", and the honest answer is always "more than we think".

How much has to be rewritten in year one? We do not need the code to be good. We need to know what it will cost to carry, which is a different question from whether it is pretty. A messy codebase that does four things is fine. A tidy one that does forty is not.

Who here is actually going to run it? This is the one that kills most deals. The price is affordable. The attention is not.

Why most of them are not worth it

Look at enough listings and a pattern shows up. A lot of what is for sale is somebody's exhausted year. The revenue is real, but it only exists because the founder posted constantly, answered every message within the hour, and ran a discount every quarter. Stop doing all that and the revenue goes with it. You are not buying a business, you are buying a job with a purchase price attached.

The ones worth having look boring from the outside. Flat revenue, no growth story, a founder who lost interest years ago and kept collecting anyway. Nobody bids those up, because there is nothing to get excited about, which is exactly the point.

And we still pass on most of them. Not because the price is wrong, usually, but because we only have so much attention and every product we take on uses some of it permanently. That is the same reason we like cheap options and small commitments: a bad domain costs ten euros a year, a bad acquisition costs a decade of someone paying attention to it.

Building is for fit, buying is for time

Where we have landed: we build when we want something that does not exist in the shape we want it, and we accept that the first year is a research project. We buy when the thing already exists, works, and we mostly want the time back.

Both end up in the same place. Something small, that earns quietly, that we intend to still own in ten years. The route matters much less than whether we still want it in year nine.
